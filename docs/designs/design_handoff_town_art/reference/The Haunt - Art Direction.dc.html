The Haunt · Art Direction Bible · v1 · draft for Kevin

A cozy town that is
quietly wrong

This document sets the visual rules for The Haunt: one house palette, one tile grammar, one lighting model, and three act-scoped variants of all of it. It exists so that art commissioned in six months still sits next to art commissioned today, and so the dread can escalate without a re-draw.

Scope of this pass: the town exterior. Farm, interiors, mine and shore inherit the palette and lighting rules but need their own tile grammar sections later. Nothing here overrides docs/design.md canon; names are still deliberately absent.

01

Resolution, tile size, and character scale

Keep 16px tiles. They are already load-bearing in the code — TileSize = 16, tile centers at (x*16+8, y*16+8), the 48×30 town, the collision polygons, the spawn markers. Moving to 32px would double every asset's cost and buy detail that a cozy sim spends most of its time not looking at. 16px is also the size the largest pool of purchasable and CC0 art is drawn at, which matters for the sourcing question below.

Tighten the viewport. 640×360 shows 40×22 tiles at once. That is a wide, map-like read: buildings are small, faces are unreadable, and the town's 48×30 footprint is almost entirely on screen at all times, which kills the sense that there is more town around the corner. Recommendation: 480×270 (30×17 tiles) in the same 1280×720 window — an integer-friendly 2.66× scale, or 960×540 window for a clean 2×. It costs one constant and a camera-limit re-check, and it roughly doubles the apparent size of every character and building.

640 × 360
40×22 tiles visible. Current. Reads as a strategy map.
480 × 270 ← use this
30×17 tiles. A character is 6% of screen height. Buildings loom.

27×15 tiles. Intimate, but the plaza no longer fits in frame.

Characters: 16×32, not 16×22. The current placeholder is 16×22 with no room for a silhouette. Move to a 16 wide × 32 tall canvas: feet anchored at the bottom row, occupying one tile of floor, overhanging one tile upward. That gives 10–12px of head — enough for a hat, hair shape, and an eye line that survives at 2× — and keeps the sprite an exact tile multiple so the atlas math stays trivial. Keep the facing encoding (0=down, 1=left, 2=right, 3=up) and mirror left from right; nobody in a cozy sim needs asymmetric handedness.

Thirty colors, two slots reserved. Every asset in the game draws from this list and nothing else. 03

Tile grammar — town exterior

Projection: flat-top three-quarter. Ground is drawn straight down — no perspective skew on grass, road or cobble. Anything vertical (buildings, fences, trees, signs, the shipping bin) is drawn in elevation, front face only, as though the camera sits slightly above and dead ahead. No side walls, ever. This is the cheapest projection to draw consistently and the only one that survives a large tile library drawn by more than one hand.

Light comes from the upper left, always. Highlights on the top and left edges of raised forms; a one-pixel ink-700 contact shadow along the bottom-right. Never a drop shadow larger than 2px — this is a daylight town, not a diorama.

Outlines: selective, never black. Terrain has no outline. Objects a player can walk behind get a 1px ink-700 outline on their lower half only, so they read against grass. Characters are fully outlined in ink-700; that outline is what makes them pop off any terrain and it is the one place the rule is absolute.

Terrain layers and their transitions

Terrain Variants Transition rule
Grass 4 base + 3 detail (clover, stones, bare patch) Base layer. Detail tiles at ≤8% frequency, never adjacent to each other.
Dirt road 3 base + wheel-rut pair 47-tile blob autotile over grass. Soft, 2px irregular edge — roads are worn, not cut.
Cobble plaza 2 base + 1 worn centre 15-tile edge set over dirt. Hard 1px stone-dark kerb — the plaza is the one built surface.
Gravel verge 2 Manual, 1 tile wide, wherever road meets a building footprint.
Tall grass / woods edge 3 + 4 corner pieces Blocking. This is what replaces the current stone map border — see §08.

Building anatomy

Every town building is assembled from the same five bands, bottom to top: foundation (1 tile, stone-base rubble), wall (2–3 tiles, clapboard in cream or masonry in stone-light), eave (1 tile, the shadow line under the roof), roof (2 tiles, slate or cedar shake), ridge (1 tile, plus a chimney where a hearth exists). The existing footprints fit this without moving a single collision cell: the town hall's x20–27, y6–11 is six rows — foundation, two wall, eave, two roof — and the general store's x8–14, y8–11 is four, so it becomes the low-slung single-storey building and reads as subordinate to the hall. That hierarchy is worth keeping.

Doors are 1 tile wide and 2 tall visually, 1 tile of collision — the existing door cells at (23,11) and (11,11) stay exactly where they are; the upper half of the door frame is drawn into the wall tile above. Doors get a warm interior glow when the building is open (see §04, and it should read against ShopHours) and a shuttered variant when closed. That is the cheapest possible "is the store open" affordance and it means the sign never has to be read twice.

Windows are where the game keeps its secrets. Every building needs three window states — lit, dark, and shuttered — as separate tiles, addressable per building. Almost every dread beat in §05 is a window state change, so build them as a swappable layer from the start rather than baking them into wall tiles.

04

Light, time of day, and season

One full-screen tint layer over the world, below the UI, driven off TenMinuteTicked and lerped between the keys below. Art is drawn at day values only; every other hour of the game is this one multiply. Interiors do not receive the tint — they get their own fixed warm key, which is what makes stepping inside at dusk feel like relief.

Key Tint Blend Feel

Point lights. Lanterns, lit windows and the store's open door punch through the tint as additive radial sprites in lantern #f2b95c — two sizes only (2-tile and 4-tile radius), and the falloff is a hand-dithered sprite, not a shader gradient, so it stays pixel-honest. Lights flicker on a 4-frame loop at 6fps with ±4% radius. Nothing in the town is lit by anything but fire; there is no electric light in this palette, which is itself a piece of characterisation.

Seasons

Four terrain sets, one lighting adjustment each. New England is the whole point of the setting, so autumn should be the showpiece and winter should be genuinely hard to look at for long.

SPRING
Green-light dominant, mud on every road edge, standing water. Wettest palette.
SUMMER
Deepest greens, dust on the road, hardest shadows. Dawn arrives an hour earlier.
AUTUMN
The showpiece. Barn-red and amber canopies, leaf litter drifting over the cobbles. Also where bile-green can hide in plain sight.
WINTER
Bone and stone only; greens vanish entirely. Dusk at 16:00. The lit windows carry the whole screen.

05

How the dread escalates

The design canon says the opening is entirely cozy and the dread arrives first through dialogue. You have asked for the art to escalate visibly by act, so the rule is: the art never leads the writing, it confirms it. A player should notice something is wrong only after an NPC has already made them uneasy, and then feel that the town has been like this the whole time.

Mechanically this is cheap if it is planned now: each act is a variant set of the same tiles at the same coordinates, swapped by a story flag. No map is rebuilt. Budget roughly 20% extra tiles per act, not a second tileset.

ACT INothing is wrong
Full warm palette. No plum, no bile-green, no bone anywhere in the town exterior. One single exception per map, placed where the player will walk past it fifty times before it matters: in town, the plaza's worn centre cobble is a slightly different shape than paving stones should be. It is never pointed at. Also: the road always curves out of frame to the west — the player can never see a horizon, only more town. That is the one piece of level-design dread that ships in Act I.
ACT IIThe details stop agreeing with each other
Greens lose 8% saturation; plum enters the shadow step of every stone ramp, so masonry shadows go faintly violet. Then four specific tells, all window and prop swaps: one house window is lit at 01:00 every night, and it is a different house each week. One building's contact shadow falls to the upper-left, against the light. The store's hanging sign gains a 2-frame idle sway with no wind anywhere else on screen. Leaf litter in autumn drifts toward the plaza centre regardless of which way the player walks.
ACT IIIThe town admits it
The palette splits: highlights push hotter toward lantern, shadows collapse into plum-black, and the mid-tones thin out — a high-contrast, low-mid image that is tiring to look at, deliberately. Green-dark becomes the dominant grass. Bile-green appears in living vegetation. Ash gathers in the cobble seams. Windows go shuttered building by building as residents give up on the evening, and on tribute nights the lantern flames burn bone instead of amber — the single most legible signal in the game that tonight is different, and it costs one recolored 4-frame sprite.

Rule of thumb for anything proposed later: if it would make a player say "that's scary," it belongs in Act III. If it makes them look twice and then move on, it is Act II. Act I gets nothing.

06

Characters

16×32, feet on the bottom row, outlined in ink-700. Silhouette carries the identity — hat, hair mass, coat length, posture — because at this size a face is four pixels and cannot. The current placeholder already has the right instinct in distinguishing NPCs by tunic color; keep that as a secondary channel, not the primary one. Two skin ramps and three hair ramps are enough for a town this small, and a limited cast whose bodies read as a family of shapes is a stronger look than variety for its own sake.

Animation counts below are the full first-pass commission. Walk cycles come first; everything else can ship as a 2-frame stand-in without the game looking broken.

07

Where the art should come from

You are undecided, so here is a recommendation rather than a menu. Split it: buy the terrain, commission the town.

Terrain, crops, tools, UI frames and generic props are solved problems. A 16px asset pack — a permissive commercial pack, or the CC0 material on OpenGameArt and Kenney's libraries — gets you a playable-looking farm and store immediately, at a cost measured in hours. Recolor whatever you use to the palette in §02 as the first act of integration; that single step is what makes bought art stop looking bought.

What no pack will ever contain is the part that makes The Haunt itself: the town's five named buildings, the cast's silhouettes, and every Act II and Act III variant. Nothing off the shelf has a tile whose shadow falls the wrong way. Commission those, from one artist, after the palette and the animation spec above are locked — a pixel artist quoting against a fixed 30-color palette and a frame table is quoting against something real, and the estimate will be both cheaper and more accurate than a quote against a vibe.

Drawing it yourself is a legitimate third answer — but only for terrain, and only if you enjoy it. Tiles are forgiving: a grass variant is an afternoon. Character animation is not; a bad walk cycle is the single fastest way to make a game read as amateur, and it is the last thing to try to learn on a project this size.

Whichever route: keep PlaceholderSprites and the procedural tile atlases working the whole way through. They cost nothing to keep, they let a new map ship before its art exists, and every asset that replaces one of them is a visible piece of progress.

First order — the town exterior

The smallest set of assets that makes the existing 48×30 town stop looking like a prototype. Everything here fits the current collision map without moving a cell.

TERRAIN · 31 TILES
4 grass base · 3 grass detail · 3 dirt · 2 wheel rut · 47-tile dirt-over-grass blob set (reducible to 13 hand-drawn with corner reuse) · 3 cobble · 15-tile cobble edge set · 2 gravel
BOUNDARY · 7 TILES
Replace the stone border ring with a woods edge: 3 tree-mass tiles, 4 corners. The map's limit should read as forest that turns you around, not as a wall — the no-leave rule deserves to be diegetic from the first hour.
TOWN HALL · 6×8 TILES
Masonry, two storeys, slate roof, cupola over the ridge. Double door at (23,11) with a lit fanlight. The tallest thing in town; the mayor's authority is architectural.
GENERAL STORE · 7×4 TILES
Clapboard, single storey, cedar shake, three windows. Door at (11,11) in open (warm glow) and closed (shuttered) states, driven by ShopHours. Hanging sign on a bracket — 2-frame sway held in reserve for Act II.
PLAZA DRESSING · 9 PROPS
Well or pump, two benches, notice board, three planters, two lamp posts with the 4-frame flame. The plaza at x22–26, y18–21 is the town's social room and currently has nothing in it — this is the highest-value dressing in the whole order.
LIGHTING · 6 SPRITES
2-tile and 4-tile dithered falloff sprites, 4-frame flame loop, and the three window states as a swappable layer. This is what buys dusk, and dusk is the screenshot the game gets remembered by.

Two open questions before anything is commissioned. The town is 48×30 with two buildings, which is roughly a quarter of the town it needs to be; adding the remaining building footprints now costs nothing and prevents a re-lay later. And the viewport decision in §01 changes what "readable" means for every tile in this order, so it should be settled first.

Nothing in this document names the town, its residents, or the malevolence. That is still yours.
